What an EDPNA Must Know

You asked about “ENDPA” — SSA’s official name is EDPNA: Eligible for Direct Payment Non-Attorney. An EDPNA is a non-attorney representative who has met SSA’s requirements and may receive SSA-authorized fees paid directly out of a claimant’s past-due benefits. A claimant can appoint an EDPNA at any level of administrative review.

Open SSA’s Official EDPNA Page

How to Become an EDPNA

  • Apply on Form SSA-1691. SSA accepts applications only February 1–28 each year. Application fee: $1,000.
  • Education: a bachelor’s degree from an accredited U.S. school, or a high school diploma / GED plus at least 4 years of relevant professional experience (work reviewing medical reports and assessing physical or mental limitations — for example teaching, counseling, social work, nursing / health care, or Title II / XVI claims work).
  • Pass SSA’s criminal background check, and attest you have not been suspended / disbarred, have no malpractice or fraud judgment or lien, and have no felony conviction.
  • Pass SSA’s written exam (below).
  • Carry continuous professional liability insurance — at least $100,000 per incident / $500,000 annual aggregate.
  • After you pass, send proof of education / experience and insurance within 12 months, then register for direct payment on Form SSA-1699.
  • Keep eligibility every year: complete SSA’s required continuing education, including one ethics course, and certify it by September 30 each year.

The 2026 exam was held remotely June 3–6, 2026, so that window has passed. The next step you can act on is the February 2027 application window — use the time between now and then to study the knowledge map on the right.

The Exam — At a Glance

  • 50 multiple-choice questions, up to 3 hours, 70% or higher to pass.
  • Taken online with a remote proctor; no one else may be in the room and your webcam stays on.
  • During the exam SSA provides the Electronic Code of Federal Regulations, the Social Security Rulings, and the EDPNA webpage to refer to. The exam does not cover Acquiescence Rulings.
  • SSA’s own sample questions cover things like education categories (marginal / limited / high school and above) and whether medical findings meet a specific Listing — for example chronic heart failure under Listing 4.02.
  • What it tests, in SSA’s words in summary: the relevant provisions of the Social Security Act, plus recent changes in SSA policy and court decisions affecting Title II and Title XVI.

Knowledge Map — What to Study

  • The two programs: Title II (SSDI, DAC, survivors — based on a work record and insured status) vs. Title XVI (SSI — need-based; countable income, resources, living arrangements, and parental deeming for children).
  • The 5-step sequential evaluation for adults: (1) working at SGA? (2) severe medically determinable impairment lasting 12 months or ending in death? (3) meets or equals a Listing? (4) can the person do past relevant work, given their Residual Functional Capacity (RFC)? (5) can they do other work, considering RFC, age, education, and work experience?
  • Children’s SSI is different: meets / equals a childhood Listing, or functionally equals a Listing across the six domains of functioning; plus parental deeming and the age-18 redetermination under adult rules.
  • The Listings (Blue Book) — Part A for adults, Part B for children — and how to match medical evidence to every element of a Listing.
  • Evidence: medical records, acceptable medical sources, opinion evidence, school and therapy records for children, function reports, and consistency / supportability of opinions.
  • Money rules: SGA, Trial Work Period, Extended Period of Eligibility, Expedited Reinstatement, IRWE, PASS, Student Earned Income Exclusion, ABLE accounts, and SSI resource limits. 2026 figures are in the lookup section below.
  • Appeals: reconsideration, ALJ hearing, Appeals Council, federal court — the 60-day deadlines, and how hearings are run (HALLEX).
  • Fees and conduct: fee agreement vs. fee petition, the rules of conduct for representatives in 20 CFR Part 404 Subpart R and Part 416 Subpart O, and ethics — boundaries, billing, privacy, and handling records.
  • SSA’s own manuals: POMS for how SSA processes claims day to day, HALLEX for hearings and appeals.

When a Client Asks… Where to Look

Find the client’s question, check the source in the middle, then confirm the facts on the right before you answer. Do not guess a dollar amount or a deadline — look it up here first.

Client questionWhere to lookWhat to check
“Can I work while I get SSDI or SSI?” SSA Red Book — work incentives and the Trial Work Period page 2026 figures (verify for the current year before quoting): SGA is $1,690/month gross for non-blind, $2,830/month if statutorily blind. A Trial Work Period month is a month over $1,210 (SSDI only; 9 months within 60 months). Then check EPE, Expedited Reinstatement, and IRWE. For SSI, work reduces the payment gradually — check the earned income exclusion and, for students under 22, the Student Earned Income Exclusion.
“My child turns 18 — what happens to SSI?” SSA’s Continuing Disability Reviews / age-18 information, plus the Adult Listings (Blue Book Part A) SSA reviews the case under the adult rules around age 18, and parental income / resources stop counting (deeming ends). A child who was denied only because of a parent’s income may qualify at 18. Start planning months before the birthday: adult medical evidence, function, and work ability are what count now.
“I was denied — what do I do?” The denial notice itself, then SSA’s appeal forms (below) Every appeal level has a 60-day deadline from the notice date (SSA assumes receipt 5 days after the date on the notice). Reconsideration: SSA-561 + SSA-3441. Hearing: request for hearing before an ALJ. Then Appeals Council, then federal court. Missing a deadline needs a written “good cause” explanation.
“Does my condition qualify?” The Listings (Blue Book) — Part A adults, Part B children A diagnosis alone is not enough. Match the medical records to every element of the closest Listing. If it does not meet a Listing, the claim is decided on function — RFC for adults, domains of functioning for children — so function reports and school / therapy records matter.
“SSA says I was overpaid.” SSA-632 (waiver request) and SSA-634 (change in repayment rate) Three separate questions: Was there really an overpayment? Was the client at fault? Would repayment be against equity / defeat the purpose? Filing a waiver or appeal on time can pause collection — check the notice for its deadline.
“Can my adult child get benefits on my record?” The DAC section below Disability began before age 22, the adult child is unmarried (with limited exceptions), and a parent is retired, disabled, or deceased and insured. See the article and checklist below.
“How much can I have in the bank on SSI?” SSI resource rules in POMS / SSA’s SSI pages SSI countable resource limit: $2,000 for an individual, $3,000 for a couple. SSDI has no resource limit. ABLE accounts and special needs trusts can protect savings in the right situation — that is a planning referral, not a guess.

DAC — Disabled Adult Child Benefits

The article you asked me to add:

DAC Benefits — What You Need to Know (Match Disability Law)

DAC in Plain Language

A short summary in my own words — read the full article above for the details:

  • DAC (SSA also calls it Childhood Disability Benefits) is paid on a parent’s earnings record, not the adult child’s own work record — that is what makes it so important for people disabled early in life with little work history.
  • Core tests: disability began before age 22, the adult child is unmarried (limited exceptions), and the parent is receiving retirement or disability benefits, or is deceased and was insured.
  • Work still matters: earnings above SGA can end eligibility. For 2026 the article gives SGA as $1,690/month — the same figure in the lookup table above.
  • Dual entitlement: someone can qualify on their own record and a parent’s. SSA does not pay both in full — the person effectively receives the higher total, with the DAC amount topping up their own benefit.
  • Medicare can follow DAC after a 24-month waiting period, which is often as valuable as the cash benefit.
  • Watch for the traps: proving the onset date before 22, old childhood medical and school records, and how marriage or work can change eligibility.

When a DAC Question Comes In

  • Ask first: When did the disability begin? Any medical or school records from before age 22?
  • Ask about the parent: retired, disabled, or deceased? Insured under Social Security?
  • Ask about marriage — past and current — before assuming eligibility.
  • Ask about any work since 18: it affects both SGA and whether the person may also be insured on their own record (dual entitlement).
  • Compare DAC against the person’s own SSDI and SSI — the best answer is sometimes a combination, and SSI / Medicaid interacts with DAC income.

Law & Official Sources — Check These First

The Law Itself

  • Social Security Act, §206 (42 U.S.C. §406) — representation and fees
  • 20 CFR Part 404 — Title II rules; Part 416 — Title XVI / SSI rules
  • 20 CFR 404.1717 / 416.1517 — the EDPNA direct-payment rules: 404.1717 · 416.1517 (Cornell LII)
  • Subpart P, Appendix 1 — the Listing of Impairments
  • Subpart R (404) / Subpart O (416) — rules of conduct for representatives

How SSA Applies the Law

  • POMS — SSA’s day-to-day processing manual. Example: POMS on appointment of a representative (GN 03910.040)
  • HALLEX — hearings, Appeals Council, and litigation procedures. Start with HALLEX I-1-0-3, who HALLEX is for
  • Social Security Rulings (SSRs) — binding policy explanations; the EDPNA exam draws on these and the eCFR
  • The Blue Book — Disability Evaluation Under Social Security: the Listings, at ssa.gov/disability/professionals/bluebook
  • The Red Book — SSA’s guide to work incentives (linked above)

Reading Order for a New Question

  1. Read the client’s SSA notice carefully — it cites the exact rule SSA used.
  2. Find the regulation (CFR) behind that rule.
  3. Check POMS for how SSA actually processes it.
  4. For a hearing issue, check HALLEX.
  5. Check for an SSR on point before giving an answer.

Stay Current — My Checklist

Every Fall

  • New COLA, SGA, Trial Work Period, and Student Earned Income Exclusion amounts for next year — update the numbers on this page
  • February 1–28: EDPNA application window (SSA-1691 + $1,000) in the year you plan to test

Every Year

  • By March 31: SSA posts the year’s required EDPNA continuing-education courses
  • By September 30: certify continuing education, including one ethics course
  • Renew professional liability insurance without a gap
  • One national conference (NADR or NOSSCR) + one Texas family / transition conference

Keep a Training Log

For every course, save: exact course title, date taken, provider, and the syllabus or a short description. SSA requires exactly those four things to certify an ethics course — keeping them for every course means you are never scrambling at the September 30 deadline.
